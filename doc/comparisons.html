ETF1100, ETB1100 and ETX1100 Content Comparison

Scope and source hierarchy

This comparison reviews three collections:

  • The complete ETF1100 Semester 1 2026 sequence in ETF1100-S1-2026/: 12 seminar decks, 11 workshop decks, tutorial materials, solutions, and Excel datasets.
  • The eight supplied ETB1100 Semester 2 2026 seminar and workshop blocks in ETB1100-S2-2026/, together with their tutorial materials.
  • The previous intensive delivery in ETX1100-NOV12-2025/, including its seminar, workshop, practice, solution, formula, notation, and revision materials.

The 2026 ETX1100 unit document remains authoritative for learning outcomes, assessment, dates, and delivery constraints. The three teaching collections are candidate sources rather than authorities of equal weight.

The comparison focuses on substantive teaching content and reusable learning design. Inherited dates, staff details, email addresses, Moodle paths, class modes, assessment reminders, and standard-semester logistics are not reusable.

Executive answer: does ETF1100 supersede ETB1100?

For development of the new ETX1100 materials, ETF1100 should supersede ETB1100 as the primary content spine. It is the most complete current collection, covers all six ETX learning outcomes, and continues the shared ETF/ETB sequence through multiple regression, spreadsheet optimisation, an integrated business case, and revision.

That is a content-development judgment, not evidence that ETF1100 formally or administratively replaces ETB1100. The supplied files do not establish any institutional relationship of that kind. Indeed, the ETB files are dated later in 2026, but the collection stops after Week 8 and is therefore incomplete for this review.

The more accurate relationship is:

  • ETF and ETB appear to be closely related versions of a common teaching design.
  • ETB Weeks 1–8 are largely a reordered and selectively revised subset of ETF Weeks 1–8.
  • ETF is the complete source set and therefore the safer base for ETX.
  • ETB remains useful as a source of alternative wording, introductory scaffolding, activities, and presentation refinements.
  • The previous ETX intensive remains useful for judging eight-week pacing and mining worked practice, but it should not determine the 2026 curriculum.

The earlier recommendation to use ETB as the base and ETX as the technical supplement is therefore superseded. The new recommendation is ETF as the content spine, ETB as a selective pedagogical supplement, and legacy ETX as a pacing and practice supplement.

Evidence of a shared ETF–ETB lineage

ETF and ETB are not substantially independent curricula. Their supplied materials repeatedly use:

  • The same chief examiner and closely related visual design.
  • The same recurring Real Estate, Marketing, Heart Disease, and related business datasets.
  • The same Excel-first approach using pivot tables, Data Analysis output, charts, and statistical functions.
  • The same progression from description to probability, inference, and regression.
  • The same or very similar stated learning outcomes and explanatory wording.
  • The same business interpretation emphasis and many of the same examples.

Their most visible structural difference is the order of two early blocks:

  • ETF teaches categorical probability in Week 3 and numerical description/distributions in Week 4.
  • ETB teaches numerical description/distributions in Week 3 and categorical probability in Week 4.

After that reversal, the sequences converge again: sampling and confidence intervals, hypothesis tests for means, inference for proportions, and simple regression.

Important differences

ETF1100: complete contemporary content spine

ETF is the only collection that supplies a coherent current path across all six ETX learning outcomes:

  1. Data types, visualisation, and descriptive analysis.
  2. Probability and distributions.
  3. Sampling, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests.
  4. Categorical and numerical relationships.
  5. Simple and multiple regression in Excel.
  6. Spreadsheet business modelling and optimisation.

Its Weeks 8–10 resolve the largest gaps in the supplied ETB set, while Weeks 11–12 provide integration and revision assets. This completeness matters more than small stylistic differences between ETF and ETB.

ETB1100: useful alternate explanations, not the governing sequence

ETB’s strengths remain valuable:

  • Beginner-friendly phrasing and transitions.
  • Strong emphasis on data quality, visual integrity, and plain-language interpretation.
  • Alternative ordering of numerical and categorical foundations.
  • Classroom prompts, polls, and recurring cases that may be more concise than the ETF versions.
  • Tutorial and workshop activities that can enlarge the practice bank.

However, the supplied collection ends at the introduction to regression. It cannot by itself establish coverage for full multiple regression, spreadsheet optimisation, the integrated case, or revision. There is no longer a sound basis for calling ETB the primary springboard for the full intensive unit.

ETX1100 NOV12 2025: useful intensive precedent with legacy curriculum

The previous ETX offering demonstrates workable compression decisions:

  • Combine visual and numerical description early.
  • Teach probability rules and distributions together.
  • Consolidate confidence intervals for means and proportions.
  • Consolidate hypothesis tests for means and proportions.
  • Preserve distinct simple- and multiple-regression blocks.
  • Reserve the final week for integration and revision.

Its limitations are equally important:

  • It gives a full week to chi-square testing, which is not named in the 2026 outcomes or developed as a core ETF topic.
  • It uses time-series forecasting as business modelling, while ETF Week 10 now provides direct evidence that LO6 concerns spreadsheet formulation, Data Tables, Solver, Goal Seek, constraints, sensitivity, and optimisation.
  • Its files mix lectures, workshops, solutions, and revision, with duplicated content and legacy administration.

ETX is therefore a pacing precedent and exercise bank, not the primary content authority.

Springboard potential for the eight-week intensive

The strongest development strategy is to start from ETF’s complete content, compress it using the previous ETX pacing model, and then inspect the corresponding ETB and ETX files for better explanations or activities.

File-level reuse priorities

Use ETF as the primary source

  • ETF Weeks 1–10 for the substantive curriculum.
  • ETF Weeks 8–9 for both simple and multiple regression rather than relying on the incomplete ETB regression coverage.
  • ETF Week 10 for the full LO6 modelling block.
  • ETF Week 11’s supermarket case and statistical-literacy workshop for integration.
  • ETF Week 12 and its tutorial/mock-exam materials for the revision bank.
  • ETF workshop and tutorial datasets as the first place to look for a matching Excel activity.

Mine ETB selectively

  • Weeks 1–2 for concise introductory, data-quality, visualisation, and normalisation explanations.
  • Weeks 3–5 for alternative transitions between numerical distributions, categorical probability, and sampling.
  • Weeks 6–7 for plain-language inference explanations and checks for understanding.
  • Week 8 for an accessible first explanation of correlation and simple regression.
  • Workshops and tutorials for additional guided practice, polls, and novice-friendly Excel instructions.

ETB slides should be selected at the explanation or activity level rather than imported as whole decks. Much of their content duplicates ETF and would create repetition.

Mine legacy ETX selectively

  • Week 1 descriptive and Excel exercises where more practice is required.
  • Week 2 probability and distribution calculations.
  • Week 3’s consolidated confidence-interval structure.
  • Week 5’s consolidated hypothesis-testing structure and exam-style questions.
  • Weeks 6–7 for extra simple- and multiple-regression output interpretation.
  • Week 8 for intensive-format revision activities.
  • Chi-square and time-series materials only as optional or replacement content if their status is formally confirmed.

Content that should not be carried forward automatically

  • A dedicated chi-square week from the previous ETX sequence.
  • A full time-series forecasting block presented as the default interpretation of LO6.
  • Duplicate ETF and ETB explanations of the same concept.
  • Both critical-value and p-value testing workflows if the assessment only expects the p-value approach.
  • Standard-semester logistics, group-assignment instructions, old Moodle paths, staff details, and class arrangements.
  • Whole solution decks placed into student-facing materials.
  • Simulation claims beyond the actual depth of ETF Week 10, whose substantive tools are Data Tables, Solver, and Goal Seek.

Final recommendation

Treat ETF1100 as the canonical teaching-content source for the new ETX1100 intensive because it is complete, current, Excel-centred, and directly aligned with all six outcomes.

Use ETB1100 as a pedagogical variation layer: select clearer explanations, prompts, visual examples, and beginner-friendly activities where they improve the ETF source. Do not use its supplied eight-week order as the target ETX curriculum.

Use ETX1100 NOV12 2025 as an intensive-delivery layer: reuse proven compression patterns, worked examples, and revision exercises, while removing legacy chi-square and forecasting emphasis unless those topics are confirmed as examinable.

In short:

  1. ETF determines what must be taught.
  2. The previous ETX offering helps determine what can fit in eight weeks.
  3. ETB helps improve how selected ideas are introduced and practised.

This hierarchy captures the genuine similarities among the collections without allowing the incomplete ETB set or the legacy ETX curriculum to displace the complete 2026 ETF evidence.
