Q21: Set a meal-kit price with Solver

Matai Kitchen, a fictional meal-prep business, tested different prices for its weekly family meal kit at a Saturday market. The workbook Week 7 pricing simulation contains 20 simulated market-day records. Use the Trial Data sheet to estimate a linear demand model with Excel’s INTERCEPT and SLOPE functions. In the Pricing Model sheet, use Solver to maximise weekly contribution profit by changing the price, subject to a price between $50 and $180 and nonnegative predicted demand. The variable cost is $24.75 per kit. Report the fitted demand equation, optimal price, predicted orders and contribution profit. Ignore fixed overhead.

Suggested answer: The fitted model is \hat Q=476.5636-2.2506p. Solver gives an optimal price of approximately $118.25, predicted demand of 210.43 kits, and weekly contribution profit of $19,675.16. Set the objective to maximise the profit cell, change the price cell, and constrain price to 50–180 with demand at least zero.

Q24: Interpret the price-and-cost sensitivity grid

The Pricing Model sheet of the simulation workbook compares prices of $95, $125, $145 and $165 across unit costs of $23, $37, $61 and $79. According to the grid, what is weekly contribution profit when the price is $145 and unit cost is $61? Describe how the grid’s best price and maximum profit change as unit cost rises.

Suggested answer: At a price of $145 and unit cost of $61, predicted demand is 150.23 kits and weekly contribution profit is $12,618.96. In the grid, the best price is $125 at unit costs $23 and $37, then $145 at costs $61 and $79. The maximum grid profit falls as unit cost rises.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 16.

Q25: Evaluate the pamphlet marketing model

A pizza company charges $25 per pizza. It pays workers $20 per five pizzas and spends $3 on ingredients per pizza. Fixed costs are $700 plus $2 per pamphlet. Pizza demand is

Q = 120 + 0.2P - 0.0003P^2,

where P is the number of pamphlets. Calculate demand, revenue, total cost and profit when P=150.

Suggested answer: Demand is 120+0.2(150)-0.0003(150^2)=143.25 pizzas. Variable cost per pizza is 20/5+3=\$7, so variable cost is 7(143.25)=\$1,002.75. Fixed cost is 700+2(150)=\$1,000. Revenue is 25(143.25)=\$3,581.25. Total cost is $2,002.75, giving profit of $1,578.50.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 20.

Q26: Assess sensitivity to pamphlet price

Use the same pizza model as Q25. A Data Table indicates that when the pamphlet price falls from $2 to $0.50, a grid value of P=300 is selected. Calculate the demand and profit at P=300 with the lower pamphlet price. How does this profit compare with the P=150, $2-per-pamphlet result in Q25?

Suggested answer: Demand is 120+0.2(300)-0.0003(300^2)=153 pizzas. Variable cost is 7(153)=\$1,071 and fixed cost is 700+0.50(300)=\$850. Revenue is 25(153)=\$3,825, so profit is $1,904. This is $325.50 higher than the Q25 result. The lower pamphlet price supports a larger advertising quantity in the Data Table grid.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 23.

Q27: Evaluate a Solver marketing-mix result

The pizza company adds Facebook advertising. Let P be pamphlets and F be Facebook ads. Demand is

Q=120+0.2P-0.0003P^2+0.3F-0.0003F^2.

Each pizza sells for $25 and has variable cost of $7. Fixed cost is 700+2P+5F. Solver returns P=148 and F=37. Calculate profit. Then hold P=148 and set F=0; how much lower is profit, to the nearest dollar?

Suggested answer: At P=148, F=37, demand is 153.7181 pizzas. Profit is 18Q-(700+2P+5F)=\mathbf{\$1,585.93}. With F=0, demand is 143.0288 and profit is $1,578.52. Removing Facebook ads lowers profit by about $7.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 24.

Q28: Calculate net present value

A business is considering an investment costing $100,000 today. It expects cash flows of $25,000 in Year 1, $40,000 in Year 2 and $55,000 in Year 3. At a discount rate of 10%, calculate NPV and state whether the investment meets an NPV-at-least-zero decision rule.

Suggested answer:

\mathrm{NPV}=-100{,}000+\frac{25{,}000}{1.10}+\frac{40{,}000}{1.10^2}+\frac{55{,}000}{1.10^3}=-\$2{,}892.56.

The NPV is negative, so the investment does not meet the NPV-at-least-zero rule at a 10% discount rate.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 28.

Q29: Compare two investments using NPV

At a 10% discount rate, compare these projects:

  • Project A: costs $100,000 today and returns $50,000 at the end of each of Years 1, 2 and 3.
  • Project B: costs $100,000 today and returns $20,000, $40,000 and $80,000 at the ends of Years 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

Calculate both NPVs. If the projects are mutually exclusive, which should the business choose under the NPV rule?

Suggested answer: Project A has NPV $24,342.60. Project B has NPV $11,344.85. Both have positive NPV, but if only one can be selected, choose Project A, which adds more value at the stated discount rate.

Topic source: Week 7 slide, page 28.

Q30: Use Goal Seek to find a break-even purchase price

Use the cash flows from Q28, but suppose the purchase price is unknown. In Excel Goal Seek, set NPV to zero by changing the initial purchase-price cell. What values should be entered for “Set cell” and “To value”, which cell should be changed, and what is the break-even purchase price?

Suggested answer: Set cell to the NPV formula cell, set “To value” to 0, and change the initial purchase-price cell. The present value of the three future cash flows at 10% is $97,107.44, so that is the break-even purchase price.
